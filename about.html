About HYDRA

A global atlas of hydroclimate variability linked to large-scale ocean–atmosphere climate modes — ten standardized climate indices, seasonal teleconnection maps, and agreement composites, all computed with common datasets, baselines, and analysis periods.

What HYDRA is

Global and regional hydroclimate variability is strongly modulated by large-scale ocean–atmosphere modes operating on interannual to multidecadal timescales. Assessments of how these modes influence hydroclimate fields — soil moisture, precipitation, drought severity, and temperature — remain scattered across studies that differ in their datasets, index definitions, methodologies, and the temporal periods chosen for analysis and for defining climatologies.

HYDRA (the HYdrological Drought and Rainfall Atlas) is a systematized global archive of climate indices, teleconnection maps, and agreement composite analyses linking ten major climate indices to hydroclimate fields derived from observational and reanalysis products. It is an open, consistent resource for exploring the large-scale drivers of global and regional hydroclimate variability, and it allows easy and consistent comparison of climate variability across modes, variables, and seasons.

Why it's needed

Hydroclimate extremes — droughts and pluvials — are among the most consequential climate hazards globally, disrupting water availability, agricultural production, ecosystem stability, and human health. These events arise from local imbalances in precipitation and evapotranspiration but are frequently driven by large-scale ocean–atmosphere modes that redistribute heat and moisture on seasonal to decadal timescales. Disentangling internal ocean–atmosphere variability from externally forced change is essential both for attributing observed hydroclimate trends and for assessing the likelihood of future extremes under continued warming.

Individual teleconnections are well documented, but comparing them across modes, variables, and regions depends on the consistency of the underlying indices and analyses. For ENSO, the choice of definition, base period, and input dataset alters which events are identified and how their teleconnections appear. For EOF-based indices such as the PDO and NAO — whose patterns depend on the analysis period and decomposition domain — the problem is even more acute.

Efforts such as the Climate Variability Diagnostics Package have standardized the modes themselves, but they focus on the indices, their SST and SLP signatures, and their fidelity in models. No systematized assessment or reference dataset had been established for how oceanic and atmospheric modes imprint on hydroclimate fields. Standardizing the indices alone is not enough: teleconnection patterns come from regressing or compositing hydroclimate fields onto an index, so they inherit every downstream choice — which observational or reanalysis product represents the variable, over what period and season, and what significance test is applied. Without holding these constant across modes, comparisons are confounded by methodology rather than physical signal.

Ten climate indices

AO and AAO are derived from geopotential height; NAO, NPI, and SAM from sea level pressure. All assessments are provided over four seasonal windows: DJF, MAM, JJA, and SON.

Hydroclimate fields

Seasonal regression maps and agreement composites are generated for the Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI), soil moisture, precipitation, and surface air temperature.

Products per index and period

Index time series

Standardized monthly and seasonal series, validated against published reference indices (all r > 0.98).

Teleconnection maps

Gridded seasonal regression and correlation maps for PDSI, soil moisture, precipitation, and temperature, with significance masks.

Agreement composites

Overlap between an index's extreme-phase years and grid-cell hydroclimate extremes, with chance-level agreement masks.

Design principles

HYDRA is intended as a working resource rather than another set of stationary datasets. Index definitions and computation methods are applied uniformly across datasets, removing inconsistencies that arise when indices are recalculated under different conventions, software implementations, or reference intervals. The reproducible code can be applied to any gridded hydroclimate product and any user-defined analysis period and baseline, so that assessments can be updated as new datasets become available — with all outputs following the same documented workflow regardless of input source.

See the Methods page for the full processing pipeline, and Usage Examples for a worked example of how HYDRA can be used in research.
