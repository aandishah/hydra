Usage Examples

A worked research example, plus technical notes on working with the indices and maps.

Example use case

ENSO and AMO hydroclimate impacts across seasons, and the sources of SST-forced seasonal variability over North America

A researcher interested in hydroclimate variability over North America — and how it depends on climate modes across seasons — will typically find the literature fragmented: separate analyses for winter and summer, each focused on the influence of a different ocean basin, and often relying on different datasets, time periods, and methodologies. This leaves open three questions:

  1. Does the dominant driver of variability switch from one basin to another across the seasonal cycle?
  2. Do the teleconnections from a given basin change in strength or pattern by season?
  3. How do the impacts over North America fit into global ocean–atmosphere patterns?

HYDRA removes the ambiguity in answering these by providing a single, internally consistent atlas — giving the consistency, comprehensiveness, and intercomparability these questions require.

Step 1 — Compare modes over North America by season

Prior literature on North American hydroclimate variability appeals to both Pacific and Atlantic forcing, so a natural starting point is the seasonal teleconnection maps of the four SST-based indices against PDSI over the long-term period.

  • ENSO — During its El Niño phase, produces a clear north–south dipole over the continent: pronounced wetting across the south paired with drying farther north. The pattern peaks in DJF and is weakest in JJA, consistent with ENSO's North American teleconnection acting mainly through the winter storm track and jet stream position.
  • PDO — Shows a similar dipole but with substantially greater reach; its northern drying signal extends much farther poleward than ENSO's, reaching the eastern and northern Alaskan coast. This may relate to the PDO's greater North Pacific expression relative to ENSO.
  • IOD — Leaves a more muted imprint. Its clearest signal emerges in SON, when the index itself is most active, bringing wetting to central North America and Alaska.
  • AMO — Strongest influence also falls in SON, wetting Alaska while drying the rest of the continent. This drying footprint reduces to a region constrained to Canada in JJA, and only to the Southwest in DJF.

Step 2 — Place the signals in global context

Moving beyond North America and focusing on ENSO and AMO globally shows how differently the two shape hydroclimate worldwide. ENSO's signal, strongest in SON and DJF, drives pronounced drying over northern South America, South Asia, and the Indo-Australian region, alongside wetting over southern South America, southwestern North America, East Africa, and the Middle East into Central Asia. The same spatial pattern persists through MAM and JJA but weakens considerably. AMO, by contrast, exerts an influence of comparable magnitude across all four seasons, peaking modestly in JJA, with a distinct fingerprint of wetting over the Sahel and India and drying across South America.

Answers to the three questions

QuestionWhat the atlas shows
Does the dominant basin switch across the seasonal cycle? Yes. ENSO and PDO govern the winter half of the year, producing the strongest and most coherent wetting/drying dipole over North America in DJF, while IOD and AMO take over in SON, when their own indices are most active.
Do teleconnections change in strength or pattern by season? Each index keeps a broadly consistent spatial signature across seasons — ENSO's north–south dipole and AMO's Alaska-wetting/continent-drying pattern both persist — but their strength, in both extent and magnitude, varies considerably, peaking in DJF for ENSO/PDO and SON for IOD/AMO, and contracting to smaller regions away from each seasonal peak.
Do North American impacts fit global patterns? Yes. The same basins that structure North American wetness and dryness leave comparable, and in some cases stronger, fingerprints elsewhere — from ENSO's drying over northern South America and South Asia to AMO's wetting over the Sahel and India. North America's response is one expression of coherent, basin-driven global teleconnections rather than an isolated pattern.

Technical usage

Getting started

A conda environment file (hydra_env.yml) is provided in the repository to help install the Python package dependencies. The interactive web platform provides a visual interface for exploring all teleconnection patterns in terms of regression maps and agreement composites. 

conda env create -f hydra_env.yml
conda activate hydra

Interpretation notes

  • Land masking. All CRU-based analyses are restricted to available land grid points, with ocean grid points masked.
  • Different baselines across index families. When working across periods, note that SST-based indices (ENSO, PDO, AMO, IOD, TPI) and HGT/SLP-based indices (NAO, NPI, AO, AAO, SAM) do not share the same baseline for the longer time comparison: the SST-index baseline is 1901–2024, while the HGT/SLP baseline is 1941–2024.
  • Indices are not fully independent. ENSO, PDO, and TPI share Pacific SST variance through their physical coupling, so their maps should not be read as independent signals.
  • SAM vs. AAO. These are often used interchangeably, but in keeping with literature definitions, HYDRA computes SAM from sea level pressure and AAO from geopotential height.
  • Significance and masking. Stippling on teleconnection maps indicates statistically insignificant regions. On agreement composites, regions below the chance-level threshold are masked, and grid cells with ambiguous response — where both the top and bottom 15th percentiles of the hydroclimate field occur among a single index phase's composite years — are shown in grey.

Data availability

All gridded teleconnection maps and agreement composite datasets are freely available at Zenodo. Climate index time series are available in both NetCDF-4 and CSV formats at the same repository. Input datasets are available from their respective providers: HadISST, CRU TS, ERA5, GLEAM, and GPCP.
